Stationary Alone, Drifting Together

Adapting to interaction-mediated nonstationarity in multi-agent reinforcement learning

Rain, sunshine and heat can change how strongly agents that share infrastructure affect one another, while each agent on its own behaves exactly as before. This page explains that kind of drift, shows where it occurs in practice, and describes how each of our four benchmarks models it.

1 · The problem

Drift that lives between agents

Many multi-agent systems are coupled through a physical medium. Vehicles share roads, solar inverters share the lines of a distribution feeder, and actuators share a power supply or a rigid body. What happens to one agent then has two parts: the effect of its own actions, and the effect of everyone else's use of the medium.

External conditions often change only the second part. Rain does not change how a car accelerates or how long its route is. It lowers the capacity of the road, so the same number of other cars now causes more delay. A car alone on the road at night hardly notices the rain. The same car in rush-hour traffic does.

We call this interaction-mediated nonstationarity. Each agent, taken alone, faces a stationary task. The fleet as a whole faces a drifting one.

What happens to one agent, in one equation

Write \(s_i\) for the part of the state that belongs to agent \(i\). Over one step, its evolution separates into a private part and a coupling part:

\[ s_i(t{+}1) \;=\; F_i\big(s_i(t),\,a_i(t)\big) \;\oplus\; C_i\big[\drv{\btheta(t)}\big]\big(\peer{s_{-i}(t),\,\ba_{-i}(t)};\ s_i(t)\big) \]
Amber: set by the external condition Blue: set by the other agents This colour code is used in every equation on the page.
\(F_i(s_i, a_i)\)
The agent on its own: how its state would evolve if no one else used the medium.
\(C_i(\peer{s_{-i}, \ba_{-i}};\, s_i)\)
The coupling: what the other agents (written \(-i\)) do to agent \(i\) through the medium.
\(\drv{\btheta(t)}\)
The coupling gains: what one unit of the others' activity costs agent \(i\) at time \(t\). External conditions act here and only here.
\(\oplus\)
How the two parts combine: by addition (an offset to injected power), by multiplication (a loss of force), or through a realised outcome (a travel time).

Three kinds of drift, and two tests that separate them

From agent \(i\)'s point of view, the environment is nonstationary whenever one ingredient of the equation above changes over time. There are three possibilities, and each has been studied by a different community. Two simple experiments tell them apart: freeze the policies of all other agents, or remove the other agents altogether.

Kind of driftWhat changesFreeze the others' policiesRemove the othersStudied in
(A) Learning-induced\(\peer{\ba_{-i}(t)}\), because the other agents are learningDrift stopsDrift stopsMulti-agent RL

Interaction-mediated drift is the only kind that survives freezing every partner and disappears for a lone agent. It survives freezing because a frozen fleet still loads the medium, and the external condition still acts on that load. It disappears for a lone agent because, with no one else on the medium, the coupling term \(C_i\) is zero whatever the condition.

Seen as one joint system, (C) is a special case of exogenous drift. What sets it apart is its structure: the drift enters only through the coupling, and it scales with the fleet's own load.

Why this drift needs its own treatment

  • The harm is a product of two factors

    Below saturation, the disturbance an agent receives in our feeder, lift and Ant benchmarks has the form

    \[ y_i^{\star}(t) \;=\; \underbrace{\drv{\sigma\,\bar k\,A(t)}}_{\text{set by the condition}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\send^{\top}\peer{\bx_i(t)}}_{\text{set by the other agents}} \]

    and routing has the same structure through the ratio of traffic volume to rain-reduced capacity. A method that is told the weather still lacks the traffic, and a method that sees the traffic still lacks the weather. With either factor missing, the executed action misses its target by an amount that no amount of training removes.

  • Only a few numbers are unknown

    Each agent knows the infrastructure layout (who shares its medium, and how strongly) and receives its peers' executed actions. Only the gains are unknown: \(r+1\) numbers per agent, where \(r\) is the number of coupling classes (three in our benchmarks), independent of the size of the fleet. These can be estimated online from the agent's own measured shortfall, which is what our method, PACT, does.

  • Reactions feed back

    A correction is itself an action on the medium, so it becomes part of the neighbours' load. Inverters that react to one another can start to oscillate, and vehicles that all follow the same accurate forecast can all switch to the route predicted to be faster and make it slower. How strongly agents should act on their estimates is therefore itself a multi-agent question.

Example 1 · URB

Cars on a shared road during the rainy season

The two examples below reduce two of our benchmarks to their smallest form. The equations are the benchmarks' own harms, restricted to a single road link and to a pair of inverters.

Car \(i\) follows a route whose busiest link has capacity \(c\), in vehicles per hour. While it travels, the other cars put a volume \(\peer{n_{-i}}\) on that link. Rain removes part of the capacity, and travel time grows with the ratio of volume to capacity, as in the volume–delay curves used in transport planning:

\[ \drv{\omega(t)} = 1 - \drv{\sigma L A(t)}, \qquad \peer{u_i(t)} = \frac{\peer{n_{-i}}}{c\;\drv{\omega(t)}}, \qquad h_i(t) = \frac{1 + \alpha\,u_i(t)}{1 + \alpha\,\peer{n_{-i}}/c} \]
\(\drv{A(t)} \in [0,1]\)
Rain intensity over a 100-day cycle: a smooth rise and fall over 50 wet days, then exactly zero on 50 dry days.
\(\drv{L} = 0.14\)
The capacity lost in heavy rain according to the Highway Capacity Manual, so \(\sigma = 1\) is heavy rain.
\(\drv{\omega(t)}\)
The fraction of the link's capacity that the rain leaves.
\(\peer{u_i(t)}\)
The load the other cars put on car \(i\)'s busiest link.
\(h_i(t)\)
How much longer the trip takes than in dry weather. The slope \(\alpha = 2.28\) is calibrated on the stock benchmark.

How it matches the definition

  • Remove othersWith no other cars, \(\peer{n_{-i}} = 0\), so \(u_i = 0\) and \(h_i = 1\) in any rain. The lone car's task is stationary.
  • Freeze othersWith every other car on a fixed route, \(h_i\) still rises through the wet season and returns to 1 on dry days. No one learns, and the task still drifts.
  • Own dynamicsThe car never changed: same route, same speed limit, same free-flow time. Only the gain \(\drv{\sigma L A(t)}\) moved, and it reaches the car only through \(\peer{n_{-i}}\).

Example 2 · MAPDN

Two inverters on one feeder on a sunny day

Inverters \(i\) and \(j\) sit on the same radial feeder and share part of their path to the substation. Reactive power injected at \(j\) shifts the voltage at \(i\) in proportion to the reactance of the lines they share. Each inverter's firmware follows a Volt/VAr curve and answers a voltage change with reactive power of the opposite sign. At night the voltage sits in the curve's flat deadband and nothing happens. Under the midday sun it sits on the sloped part, and every injection by the neighbour provokes a reaction:

\[ \Delta v_i(t) = S_{ij}\;\peer{q_j(t{-}1)}, \qquad S_{ij} = \sum_{b\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(i)\,\cap\,\mathrm{path}(j)} \frac{X_b}{V_b^{2}} \]
\[ d_i(t) = -\,\drv{\sigma k A(t)}\;\bar s_i\;\Delta v_i(t), \qquad q_i^{\ex}(t) = q_i^{\sent}(t) + d_i(t) \]
\(S_{ij}\)
Voltage sensitivity of the shared lines (linearised DistFlow): \(X_b\) and \(V_b\) are the reactance and nominal voltage of branch \(b\).
\(\drv{k}\)
The Volt/VAr slope. \(\sigma = 1\) is the IEEE 1547 Category B default curve, and \(\sigma = 3\) the steepest curve the standard allows.
\(\drv{A(t)}\)
The solar profile: \(\sin^2\) between 06:00 and 18:00, exactly zero at night.
\(\bar s_i\)
The inverter's rating; its reaction is proportional to its size.
\(q_i^{\sent},\ q_i^{\ex}\)
The reactive power the inverter was told to inject, and what it actually injects.

How it matches the definition

  • Remove othersWithout \(j\) there is no shared path, so \(\Delta v_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 0\) at any hour and any severity.
  • Freeze othersWith \(q_j\) held fixed, \(d_i\) still swings from zero at night to its largest value at noon.
  • Own dynamicsThe inverter hardware is unchanged. Only the gain \(\drv{\sigma k A(t)}\) moved.

This example also shows why reactions matter. Inverter \(j\) reacts to \(i\) in the same way, so each reaction becomes the input to the other's next one. For the whole fleet, with no compensation,

\[ \mathbf q^{\ex}(t) = \mathbf q^{\cmd}(t) \;-\; \drv{\sigma k A(t)}\;G\;\peer{\mathbf q^{\ex}(t{-}1)}, \qquad G = \operatorname{diag}(\bar{\mathbf s})\,S, \qquad \Theta(t) = \drv{\sigma k A(t)}\;\rho(G) \]

where \(\rho(G)\) is the spectral radius of \(G\). The mutual reactions die out when the loop gain \(\Theta(t)\) is below 1 and grow when it is above 1: the fleet starts to oscillate, which grid engineers call hunting. For two identical inverters \(\rho(G) = \bar s\, S_{ij}\). A single inverter has \(\rho(G) = 0\) and never hunts.

In both examples the agent's own dynamics never change, the condition acts only through the other agents, and the effect scales with how heavily they use the medium. The same structure appears in all four benchmarks.

2 · The benchmarks

Four benchmarks, one structure

Each benchmark is used exactly as published when \(\sigma = 0\). Raising \(\sigma\) strengthens an external condition whose effect on an agent scales with the other agents' load on the shared medium. The reward function is never modified: agents earn less only because the physics delivers less.

Every instance declares the same four objects.

M · medium
What the agents use together, and the load \(\phi_j\) that each user \(j\) puts on it.
W · coupling
A fixed operator computed from the infrastructure layout: which agents affect which, and how strongly.
D · driver
An external condition \(\drv{A(t)} \in [0,1]\), from no effect (0) to full effect (1). No agent can influence it.
H · harm
How the condition, at intensity \(\drv{\sigma A(t)}\), turns the peers' load into a disturbance.

At severity \(\sigma\), the action \(a_i\) that agent \(i\) sends is executed as

\[ a_i^{\ex}(t) = h\big(\drv{\sigma A(t)},\ \peer{\bx_i(t)};\ a_i(t)\big), \qquad \peer{\bx_i(t)} = \Lambda_i\big(\bx_i(t{-}1),\ \{\phi_j(a_j^{\ex}(t{-}1))\}_{j\neq i};\ W\big) \in \R^{r} \]

The channels \(\peer{\bx_i}\) summarise the other agents' executed load through \(W\), one channel per coupling class, possibly filtered over time. They are exactly zero when agent \(i\) is alone. The harm map does nothing when either factor is absent, \(h(0, \bx; a) = h(\xi, \mathbf 0; a) = a\), which is what makes both tests of Section 1 hold by construction. In routing the harm acts on the realised travel time instead of the action.

All four drivers share one shape

Each driver is a smooth bump over the active half of its cycle and exactly zero over the other half:

\[ \drv{A(t)} = \begin{cases} \sin^2\!\big(\pi\,\varphi(t)/w\big), & \varphi(t) \lt w,\\[2pt] 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad \varphi(t) = \frac{(t - t_0) \bmod T}{T}, \qquad w = \tfrac12 \]

The zero half is a built-in control: on those steps every benchmark is the unmodified original at every \(\sigma\). The agents are told neither the period nor the phase.

Where the phenomenon comes fromURB · routing in the rain

  • Harm on travel time
  • No inverse: route steering
  • σ = 1: HCM heavy rain

Connected autonomous vehicles choose routes through a real road network, simulated in SUMO, alongside human drivers that no agent controls (Akman et al., 2025). We use the saint_arnoult network: 222 travellers, of whom 89 are controlled, 745 links, 215 origin–destination pairs and 4 candidate routes per traveller. Each simulated day is one decision.

Origin of the phenomenon

Rain and traffic: measured by traffic engineers, not constructed by us

Rain lowers the number of vehicles a road can serve per hour, because drivers keep longer headways and accelerate more gently. The Highway Capacity Manual tabulates this effect as weather adjustment factors for capacity, and field studies of freeways report the same pattern (Smith et al., 2004; Agarwal et al., 2005). In heavy rain, capacity falls by about 14%. That is the value we use for \(\sigma = 1\).

Lost capacity only costs time when traffic needs it. On an empty road at night, a driver in heavy rain loses almost no time to congestion, because the capacity that remains is still far more than one car needs. At rush hour the same rain produces queues. The extra delay a driver suffers is therefore set jointly by the weather and by the other drivers. This is interaction-mediated nonstationarity as it occurs in everyday traffic. We first observed the structure in URB, and it is the model for the definition we apply to the other three benchmarks.

MMedium

Road links

Agent \(i\)'s load is taken on the busiest link of its chosen route: the flow of other vehicles over the capacity that the rain leaves.

\[ \peer{u_i}(\ba;\bomega) \;=\; \max_{e\,\in\,a_i}\ \frac{\peer{\sum_{j\neq i} O_{ij}\,\ind{e\in a_j}}}{T_i\;c_e\;\drv{\omega_e}} \]
\(\ind{e \in a_j}\)
Whether traveller \(j\)'s route uses link \(e\).
\(O_{ij}\)
Temporal overlap: whether \(i\) and \(j\) are on the road at the same time.
\(T_i\)
The duration of \(i\)'s trip, which turns a vehicle count into a flow.
\(c_e\)
Capacity of link \(e\): number of lanes × 600 vehicles per hour.
WCoupling

Who shares which link, and when

Link sharing weighted by temporal overlap and capacity, read from the road network and the departure schedule before any training.

DDriver

Rain over a 100-day wet/dry cycle

Rain leaves link \(e\) a fraction \(\omega_e\) of its capacity:

\[ \drv{\omega_e(t)} = \clip\big(1 - \drv{\sigma L A(t)}\,\eta_e,\ \underline{\omega},\ 1\big), \qquad L = 0.14 \]

\(\eta_e \in [0.4, 2.0]\) is a per-link rain sensitivity derived from the link's design speed, so faster roads lose more capacity, and the floor \(\underline{\omega}\) keeps every link open.

HHarm

A longer trip

The travel time returned by the simulator is scaled by the delay factor of the rain, relative to dry weather:

\[ \tau_i = h_i\,\tau_i^{\mathrm{sim}}, \qquad h_i = \frac{1 + \alpha_h\,\peer{u_i}\big(\ba;\drv{\bomega(t)}\big)}{1 + \alpha_h\,\peer{u_i}(\ba;\mathbf 1)} \;\ge\; 1 \]

This is the Bureau of Public Roads volume–delay curve used throughout transport planning, in its linear form because URB operates well below capacity. The slope \(\alpha_h = 2.28\) is calibrated once on the stock benchmark (a mean excess delay of 0.50 at a mean busiest-link ratio of 0.219). A delay cannot be undone once the route is chosen, so the harm has no inverse. PACT instead biases the route choice toward routes predicted to be less delayed.

  • σ = 0 · dry day\(h_i = 1\) exactly. Verified on all 100 days of the cycle; the 50 dry days are unaffected at every \(\sigma\).
  • Lone vehicleEvery numerator is zero, so \(h_i = 1\). In SUMO a lone vehicle reads exactly zero peer load.
  • Frozen partnersThe 133 human drivers follow fixed, selfish policies, and their load still turns into more or less delay as the rain comes and goes.
  • Monotone\(h_i\) never decreases as \(\sigma\) grows.

Power distributionMAPDN · voltage control under the midday sun

  • Harm on reactive power, additive
  • Inverse: subtract
  • σ = 1: IEEE 1547 default curve

Photovoltaic inverters on a radial distribution feeder regulate voltage by injecting or absorbing reactive power \(q_i\) (Wang et al., 2021). We use the 141-bus, 12.5 kV feeder with 22 inverters. The goal is to keep every bus voltage within 0.95–1.05 per unit.

In the stock benchmark each inverter delivers exactly the reactive power it is told to. Deployed inverters also run an autonomous Volt/VAr curve in their firmware, as specified by IEEE 1547-2018, and grid codes such as California Rule 21 and AS/NZS 4777.2 require it to be active. The operator can move the curve's reference voltage remotely. On the sloped part of the curve this amounts to dispatching a reactive-power level, while the curve keeps reacting to the terminal voltage around it. We model the dispatched level as the agent's action and the curve's reaction as the harm. The terminal voltage is moved by the reactive power of every other inverter on the shared lines, so the curves respond to one another. On feeders with many inverters this interaction is a known cause of voltage oscillation (Jahangiri & Aliprantis, 2013; Farivar et al., 2013; Andrén et al., 2015).

The sun sets how strong the interaction is. At night the voltage sits inside the curve's flat deadband and the firmware does nothing. Around midday, high solar output raises voltages onto the sloped part of the curve, and the same injections from the neighbours provoke stronger reactions.

MMedium

Feeder lines

Agent \(i\)'s load is the voltage change that its peers' last injections cause at its terminal, split by conductor class \(m\) (heavy, medium and light lines, by resistance per kilometre):

\[ \peer{x_{m,i}(t)} = \sum_{j} S_m[i,j]\;\peer{q_j^{\ex}(t{-}1)} \]
WCoupling

Voltage sensitivity of the shared lines

\[ S_m[i,j] = \sum_{\substack{b\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(i)\,\cap\,\mathrm{path}(j)\\ \cls(b)=m}} \frac{X_b}{V_b^{2}}, \qquad S_m[i,i] = 0 \]

The linearised DistFlow sensitivity (Baran & Wu, 1989): how strongly reactive power at \(j\) moves the voltage at \(i\) through the class-\(m\) lines on their common path to the substation. It is read from the network model before any training.

DDriver

A clear-sky solar day

\(A(t) = \sin^2\) between 06:00 and 18:00 and exactly zero at night, on 3-minute steps (480 per day). The 241 steps with \(A = 0\) reproduce the stock task at every \(\sigma\).

HHarm

An unrequested reaction

Each inverter reacts to the voltage change its neighbours cause, with a Volt/VAr slope that the driver scales:

\[ d_i(t) = \bar s_i\,\clip\Big(-\drv{\sigma k A(t)} \sum_m \send_m\,\peer{x_{m,i}(t)},\ -q_{\mathrm{sat}},\ q_{\mathrm{sat}}\Big), \qquad q_i^{\ex} = \clip\big(q_i^{\sent} + d_i,\ -\bar q_i,\ \bar q_i\big) \]
\(\drv{k}\)
Reaction gain. \(\sigma = 1\) is the IEEE 1547-2018 Category B default slope (0.44 per-unit reactive power over 0.06 per-unit voltage); \(\sigma = 3\) is the steepest curve the standard allows, so the experiments stay within the standard.
\(\send_m\)
Class gains, unknown to the agents.
\(q_{\mathrm{sat}} = 0.44\)
The curve's own reactive-power limit, in units of rating.
\(\bar q_i\)
Capability \(\big(\bar s_i^2 - (p_i^{\mathrm{pv}})^2\big)^{1/2}\) at active power \(p_i^{\mathrm{pv}}\).

The reaction adds to the inverter's own action, so a correct estimate cancels it by subtraction. As in Example 2, reactions feed back through the lines: on this feeder \(k\rho(G) = 0.675\), so the midday threshold is \(\sigma_c = 1.48\) and the uncompensated fleet is past it at \(\sigma = 3\).

  • Lone inverterReads exactly zero harm at \(\sigma \in \{0.5, 1, 3, 100\}\).
  • σ = 0 · nightReproduces the stock task bit for bit at every driver value, and at every \(\sigma\) on the steps with \(A(t) = 0\).
  • Frozen partnersThe harm still varies over the day.
  • Type (B) controlWhen the sun acts on each inverter directly, an isolated inverter feels it. The interaction-mediated harm it does not.

Cooperative roboticsVMAS Balance · lifting from a shared supply

  • Harm on force, multiplicative
  • Inverse: divide
  • σ = 1: stated calibration

Four agents lift a beam carrying a package toward a goal (Bettini et al., 2022). If the beam tilts too far, the package falls, the episode ends and the team receives a penalty of −10. The difficulty is agreement: when one support pushes harder than the others, the beam rotates.

We add a shared supply, as in a synchronised hydraulic lift, where several jacks raise one load from a single power pack. When several jacks draw at once, the supply pressure sags and every jack delivers less force than it commanded. How much it sags for a given draw changes during operation: as the hydraulic fluid warms over a shift, its viscosity falls, internal leakage rises and the supply becomes softer. A jack alone on the supply receives full pressure at any temperature. In the benchmark this condition follows the same periodic profile as URB's wet/dry cycle.

MMedium

The supply

Each agent's load on the supply is the magnitude of its executed force. The channels filter the peers' load over time, because pressure does not respond instantly:

\[ \peer{x_{m,i}(t)} = \zeta\,x_{m,i}(t{-}1) + (1-\zeta)\sum_{j\neq i:\ \cls(j)=m} W_{ij}\,\peer{\lVert a_j^{\ex}(t{-}1)\rVert}, \qquad \zeta = 0.9 \]
WCoupling

Distance between actuators

\[ W_{ij} = \frac{\recv_{\cls(i)}}{1 + \big(\lVert \mathrm{pos}_i - \mathrm{pos}_j\rVert/\lambda\big)^2}, \qquad W_{ii} = 0 \]

Actuators closer together couple more strongly. \(\recv_{\cls(i)} \in \{1.6,\ 1.0,\ 0.4\}\) is how much droop an actuator of hardware class \(\cls(i)\) feels, and \(\lambda = 0.35\) is the length scale. Both are public.

DDriver

A periodic softening of the supply

A 100-step cycle with the shared shape: active over the first half, exactly zero over the second (51 of every 100 steps have \(A = 0\)). The clock keeps running across episodes.

HHarm

Lost force

The droop scales the delivered force down:

\[ \delta_i(t) = \clip\Big(\frac{\drv{\sigma L A(t)}}{x_{\mathrm{ref}}}\sum_m \send_m\,\peer{x_{m,i}(t)},\ 0,\ \delta_{\max}\Big), \qquad a_i^{\ex} = \clip_{\mathrm{box}}\big((1-\delta_i)\,a_i^{\sent}\big) \]
\(\send_m\)
How much a class-\(m\) actuator's draw costs the others, \((0.2,\ 1.0,\ 1.8)\). Unknown to the agents.
\(x_{\mathrm{ref}}\)
A reference load that fixes the meaning of \(\sigma\).
\(\delta_{\max} = 0.9\)
The droop limit: a support never delivers less than 10% of its command.

\(\sigma = 1\) is a stated calibration: at the driver's peak, with every peer at full effort, the droop reaches 0.14, URB's heavy-rain value, so that severities are comparable across benchmarks. The droop grows with the peers' forces, so it penalises exactly the coordinated pushing the task rewards, and the support that droops most tips the beam first. Dividing the command by one minus the predicted droop restores the commanded force, the same kind of feed-forward a lift controller applies.

  • Lone actuatorExactly zero droop at any \(\sigma\).
  • σ = 0 · A = 0Bit-identical to the stock scenario, and at every \(\sigma\) on the steps with \(A(t) = 0\).
  • Frozen partnersThe droop still varies over the cycle, from 0 to 0.018 in the self-test.

LocomotionMulti-Agent MuJoCo Ant · four legs on one torso

  • Harm on joint torque, additive
  • Inverse: subtract
  • σ = 1: stated calibration

Multi-Agent MuJoCo splits the MuJoCo Ant into agents (Peng et al., 2021). In the 4×2 configuration, each of four agents is one leg and controls the torques of its hip and ankle, each in \([-1, 1]\). The reward is the stock Ant reward: forward progress, minus control and contact costs, plus a survival bonus.

All four legs are bolted to one torso. When one leg drives hard, the torso reacts, and part of that reaction reaches the other legs' joints as torque they did not command. This coupling is part of the robot's rigid-body dynamics and is already present in stock Ant. What we add is drift in how strongly it is transmitted, driven by the temperature of the drivetrain. A cold drivetrain is stiff and firmly preloaded, and it holds each joint against the reaction. As the gearboxes and bearings warm during a run, lubricant viscosity falls, preload drops and backlash opens, so more of a neighbour's push leaks through to a joint. No leg controls the temperature, and a leg alone on the torso, with the others slack, feels nothing from them at any temperature.

MMedium

The torso

The elements of the medium are the eight joints, and each joint's load is the torque it actually delivered at the previous step, which the legs broadcast. Compliance and backlash spread a reaction over a few control steps, so the channels are filtered with memory \(\rho = 0.8\):

\[ \mathbf z_m(t) = \rho\,\mathbf z_m(t{-}1) + (1-\rho)\,G_m\,\peer{\boldsymbol\tau^{\ex}(t{-}1)} \in \R^{8} \]
\[ \mathbf e_i(t) = \frac{\mathbf z(t)\big|_i}{\big\lVert \mathbf z(t)\big|_i \big\rVert}, \quad \mathbf z = \textstyle\sum_m \mathbf z_m, \qquad \peer{x_{m,i}(t)} = \big\langle\, \mathbf z_m(t)\big|_i,\ \mathbf e_i(t) \,\big\rangle \]

Here \(\big|_i\) keeps agent \(i\)'s two joints. \(\mathbf e_i\) is the direction in the agent's two-joint action space along which the reaction arrives, and \(x_{m,i}\) is its size along load path \(m\).

WCoupling

The robot's own inverse inertia

The coupling operator is taken from the robot itself. \(\kappa\) is the joint-space inverse inertia \(|M^{-1}|\) of the Ant at its nominal pose, read out of the MuJoCo model: entry \(\kappa_{pq}\) says how strongly a unit torque at joint \(q\) accelerates joint \(p\).

\[ G_m[p,q] = \ind{\cls(p,q)=m}\;\recv_p\;\kappa_{pq}\;\ind{\mathrm{leg}(p)\neq\mathrm{leg}(q)}, \qquad \kappa = \big|M^{-1}\big|_{\text{nominal pose}} \]

The index \(m\) runs over three load paths: hip ← hip, ankle ← ankle and hip ↔ ankle. \(\recv_p\) is a declared per-actuator susceptibility with mean 1 and spread 0.35, standing for build tolerance and wear. The last factor removes pairs of joints on the same leg, so an agent never disturbs itself.

Hip ↔ ankle, any two joints0.000

The structure is not what a distance rule would suggest. Diagonal ankles have parallel axes and couple about six times more strongly than adjacent ones. At the nominal pose no hip torque accelerates any ankle, because hip axes are vertical and ankle axes horizontal. The hip ↔ ankle path is therefore exactly zero and is dropped, which leaves two live channels.

DDriver

Drivetrain temperature

A 20,000-step cycle: a \(\sin^2\) bump over the warm half and exactly zero over the cold half. At the simulator's 0.05 s control step, one cycle lasts about 17 minutes, the time scale over which a drivetrain warms. The clock keeps running across episodes. \(A(t)\) changes by at most \(3.1\times10^{-4}\) per step, so it is slow compared with control, yet not constant within a 1000-step episode.

HHarm

Unrequested joint torque

The reaction is added to the torque the leg sends, along the direction \(\mathbf e_i\):

\[ \mathbf d_i(t) = \frac{\drv{\sigma L A(t)}}{x_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Big(\sum_m \send_m\,\peer{x_{m,i}(t)}\Big)\,\mathbf e_i(t), \qquad \ba_i^{\ex}(t) = \clip\big(\ba_i^{\sent}(t) + \mathbf d_i(t),\ -1,\ 1\big) \]
\(\drv{L} = 0.14\)
The severity scale shared with the other benchmarks.
\(\send_m\)
\((1.5,\ 0.9,\ 0.6)\) for hip ← hip, ankle ← ankle and hip ↔ ankle. Hips attach directly to the torso and load each other hardest. Unknown to the agents.
\(x_{\mathrm{ref}} = 7.73\)
The value of \(\sum_m \send_m x_{m,i}\) when every peer applies full torque with random signs.

\(\sigma = 1\) is a stated calibration: at the driver's peak, with every peer at full torque, the disturbance reaching an agent is 0.14 of its torque range (measured: 0.1397). No published constant exists for drivetrain warm-up, so we reuse URB's heavy-rain value to keep severities comparable across benchmarks.

The disturbance is applied where the simulator executes the torques, below the reward. The control cost is charged on the torque the policy commanded, so the reward function is the stock one, and the ant earns less only because it walks worse. The disturbance is a torque in the agent's own action space, so a correct estimate is subtracted from the command and the delivered torque then equals the commanded one exactly. The agent measures its shortfall as \(y_i = \langle \ba_i^{\ex} - \ba_i^{\sent},\, \mathbf e_i\rangle\), which a servo reads through its motor current.

  • Lone agentWhen one agent owns all eight joints, every channel is exactly zero and the disturbance is zero at \(\sigma = 3\).
  • Frozen partnersThe disturbance still follows the warm and cold halves of the cycle.
  • FeedbackThe channels are built from delivered torques, which include the disturbance, so the disturbance feeds into its own next value, as on the feeder.

3 · Questions about the design

What the benchmarks do and do not show

In every benchmark the harm is a gain set by an external condition, multiplied by the load that the other agents put on the medium, and PACT's regressor is built from that same product. This section takes up the questions that usually follow: whether PACT is simply handed the answer, whether its use of the coupling operator is an unfair advantage, what remains hard when its model is right, and how realistic each harm is.

Form

The product form comes from the domains.

Traffic engineering, power engineering and rigid-body mechanics already describe these effects as a gain that depends on conditions, applied to a load carried by a known network.

Information

PACT uses only what the environment exposes.

The operator \(W\) is computed from the published layout, and the other inputs are the peers' broadcast actions and the agent's own shortfall. A learner told the true driver still falls short of PACT.

Difficulty

Knowing the form is not enough.

A baseline with an exact linear model, the same measurement and the same estimator still falls behind PACT, because identification and feedback remain hard.

Realism

Realism differs by benchmark.

URB and MAPDN are anchored to published constants. VMAS and the Ant use a physical mechanism with a stated calibration, and the VMAS supply is our own construction.

Is PACT simply given the answer?

On the feeder, the lift and the Ant, the harm is exactly linear in PACT's channels below saturation (Proposition C.2 of the paper). The form is not tailored to the method. It is how each field already writes the effect:

PACT is given the layout of the network and its peers' executed actions, and it estimates the gains online from its own measured shortfall. The same product is what defines the class: remove the peers and it vanishes, freeze them and the gain still moves. We first met the structure in traffic, where it is measured, and designed PACT for the class it defines.

Is using the operator W an unfair advantage?

No. PACT uses only what the environment exposes, and the same inputs are open to every method.

PACT works from three inputs: the infrastructure layout, the executed actions its peers broadcast, and its own measured shortfall. The operator \(W\) is computed from the layout before training: from the road network and the departure schedule on URB, from the feeder's line reactances on MAPDN, from the actuators' positions and hardware classes on VMAS, and from the robot's own model on the Ant. It records who can affect whom through the medium, and how strongly the structure transmits. It does not contain the gains, the severity \(\sigma\), the driver \(\drv{A(t)}\), or the driver's period and phase, and PACT is given none of these.

A known layout is also a realistic input. The people who operate these systems have it: a utility knows its feeder's impedances, a traffic authority its road network, a robot maker its robot's inertia. What they cannot know in advance is how strongly the layout transmits under the day's conditions, and that is the part PACT estimates.

The baselines receive the same kinds of input, in the form their designs call for. On VMAS every learner observes its own shortfall, and on MAPDN every learner also observes the channels, which are the peers' actions weighted by \(W\). The mean-field learners receive the peers' actions, and URLS and ESO use the shortfall exactly as PACT does. The oracle run, Oracle-A(t), goes further: it receives the one quantity PACT is never given, the true driver \(\drv{A(t)}\), on top of everything a learner normally observes. Methods that make no use of the layout do so by design, since they have no place for a known coupling structure. That is a property of those methods, not a restriction of the benchmark, and any method is free to use the layout.

  • An exact model with the same data still falls behind

    URLS runs PACT's own recursion, with the same forgetting, trust and correction, on the peers' raw actions. On the feeder its model is also exact below saturation, since every coefficient is proportional to \(\drv{A(t)}\). It must identify up to \(n\) coefficients where PACT identifies \(r+1\), and the learners' actions do not vary enough for that. Between two correct models, the declared structure decides.

  • Knowing the driver is not enough

    In every benchmark the gains move together with \(\drv{A(t)}\), so \(\drv{A(t)}\) is the only quantity that drifts. Oracle-A(t) is told \(\drv{A(t)}\) at every step and still does not improve on its host, because the harm is \(\drv{A(t)}\) multiplied by the peers' load, and the load changes whenever the co-learners change.

  • The right measurement, used as feedback, makes things worse

    The disturbance observer uses the same shortfall that PACT measures, but it learns each reaction one step late. In the feeder's feedback loop this lowers the instability threshold below that of the uncompensated fleet (the observer proposition in Appendix D). On the 6-inverter feeder at \(\sigma = 3\), with a scripted droop host, its voltage loss is 4.32 times the unharmed host's, against 4.21 with no compensation at all; PACT's is 1.04 and the oracle's 1.00.

If the model is right, what remains hard?

Three things, and a correct model class removes none of them.

  • The regressor comes from learners, and it runs dry

    Identification needs the peers' actions to vary, and convergent learning removes that variation (the excitation lemma in Appendix C). When we estimated the coupling operator itself instead of declaring it, the fitted coefficient on URB was 0.13 ± 5.0 and never significant in 5004 windows.

  • Acting on the estimate changes what is estimated

    Every correction adds to the neighbours' load. At full trust 2 of 5 runs became unstable, and 1 of 5 at trust 0.95 (the trust sensitivity table in Appendix B). If the task were only to track a few numbers, full trust would be best.

  • The outcome is not guaranteed

    On VMAS PACT reaches 83% of the unharmed return and closes a little over half of the gap. On the Ant, PACT and its host end at the same return, and the gain is in sample efficiency.

How realistic is each harm?

Each benchmark combines parts taken from its domain with parts we added to create the drift. The table separates the two.

Two details matter when reading the results. SUMO has no model of rain, so the URB delay is applied to recorded travel times rather than inside the car-following dynamics. It is applied to every traveller, human drivers included; the benchmark keeps the human drivers' policies fixed, so for them only the reported times change.

On MAPDN the agent's action plays the role of the curve's reference voltage. On the sloped part of an IEEE 1547 curve, moving the reference voltage by \(\Delta V\) shifts the reactive power by \(k\,\Delta V\), so a dispatched level with the curve reacting around it matches how the standard lets operators steer a fleet of inverters. The same linear loop is the standard model for analysing local Volt/VAr stability (Farivar et al., 2013). Our curve reacts only to the voltage that the peers cause. A complete curve would also react to load and solar variation, which adds type (B) drift on top of type (C).
